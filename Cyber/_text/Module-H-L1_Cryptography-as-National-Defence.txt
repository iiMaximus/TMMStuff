    ➢ Defensive capability: Protect state, military, diplomatic, and
      industrial secrets.
    ➢ Offensive/intelligence capability: Read, alter, or exploit other
      people's secrets.
    ➢ Sovereign capability: Do not blindly depend on uncontrollable
      algorithms, products, standards, hardware, firmware, or
      suppliers.

  Weak encryption may be worse than no encryption

    Mary and Babington trusted the cipher. Because they trusted it,
    they wrote explicitly. Because they wrote explicitly, the decrypted
    letters became evidence.
    National defence lesson:
    ➢ Weak cryptography (or compromised communication channel
       deemed secure) can change behaviour in ways that help the
       adversary.
              •    No encryption: Users may self-censor.
              •    Strong encryption: Users can communicate securely if implementation
                   and procedures are sound.
              •    Weak encryption believed to be strong: Users speak openly while
                   the adversary reads silently.

  A cryptanalytic breakthrough is a
  strategic asset
                   Breakthrough + secrecy = intelligence advantage
                   Breakthrough + disclosure = adversary adaptation

    Once a state can break a system, it faces a choice:
    ➢ publish the discovery ⟹ not convenient
      to the interests of the state
    ➢ use it secretly, to preserve the advantage
      and prevent the adversary from adapting
    This is where cryptography becomes statecraft.
    This creates a structural tension between science and intelligence:
    ➢ public science rewards disclosure
    ➢ intelligence rewards silence

  The Zimmermann Telegram: when
  cryptanalysis enters grand strategy
    January 1917: German diplomatic message to Mexico
    Germany proposed that, if the United States entered the war, Mexico should join
    Germany against the U.S.
    In return, Germany suggested support for Mexico to recover:
    ➢ Texas
    ➢ New Mexico
    ➢ Arizona
    From a German perspective, the logic was strategic distraction: if the United
    States had to worry about a war on its southern border, it would be much harder
    for Washington to send forces to Europe quickly.
    British intelligence intercepted and decrypted the message.
    Its disclosure helped shift U.S. public opinion and policy.

A.Y. 2025 - 2026
  Cryptographic sovereignty: who controls
  the tools that protect state secrets?
    Cryptographic sovereignty is not only about using encryption.
    For national defence, a state must be able to trust and govern:
    ➢ which algorithms and standards are used;
    ➢ who implements and verifies them;
    ➢ how keys are generated, stored, rotated and destroyed;
    ➢ which hardware and firmware execute cryptographic operations;
    ➢ who owns, updates and maintains the products;
    ➢ how operators use the system (do they reuse keys, bypass procedures, use
         predictable configurations?).
    Cryptographic sovereignty is the ability to protect state
    secrets without blind dependence on actors outside national
    control.

    Crypto AG was a Swiss supplier of encryption machines used by governments

    The first major breakthrough against military Enigma

  The Polish breakthrough combined four elements:
  1. Intelligence input: French intelligence obtained German Enigma material

Bletchley Park, 1939–1946
Chosen as Britain’s secret codebreaking centre
just before World War II, Bletchley Park became
home to “Government Code and Cypher School”
GC&CS. During 1940–42, Bletchley changed
from a small secret unit into an industrial-scale
intelligence operation. Intercepted enemy
wireless traffic came from “Y stations,” was
processed in huts and blocks around the estate,
and became Ultra intelligence.
By 1943 - 44, Bletchley had expanded massively.
 Staff peaked at over 9,000, many of them women, and the work moved beyond Enigma to systems
 such as the German Lorenz cipher. The need to attack Lorenz led to Colossus, delivered to
 Bletchley in early 1944, one of the landmark machines in the birth of modern electronic computing.
 At the end of the war in 1945, much of the workforce was released, and staff remained bound by
 secrecy. GCHQ records note that some wartime staff were told, after hostilities ceased, that there
 was “no further work” for them in the organisation.
 Bletchley Park’s life as the main codebreaking centre effectively ended in 1946, when the post-war
 signals-intelligence organisation moved to Eastcote; GC&CS evolved into what became GCHQ.
 The site later had various government and educational uses, declined badly, and was eventually43
 preserved as the Bletchley Park museum.

  If you act too perfectly, the adversary
  learns
    Using intelligence can reveal that you have intelligence.
    Ultra created an operational dilemma:
    ➢ Direct action: tactical advantage / risk exposing the source if act too visibly
    ➢ Covered action: plausible explanation / source preserved
    ➢ No action: tactical cost / long-term access preserved
    If every Allied action was too accurate, Germany could infer that
    Enigma was compromised. Ultra often had to be used indirectly:
    • sent reconnaissance aircraft to simulate visual observation;
    • false sightings;
    • attack only some targets, not all of them;
    • sometimes delayed or avoided action.

  Sovereign alternatives: China’s SM standards
    Not every state wants to rely on the same cryptographic trust stack.
    China has developed its own commercial cryptographic standards, often called
    SM or ShangMi (meaning “commercial cryptography”) algorithms:
    ➢ SM2: elliptic-curve public-key cryptography;
    ➢ SM3: cryptographic hash function;
    ➢ SM4: block cipher for symmetric encryption;
    ➢ ZUC: stream cipher, used in mobile-communication contexts;
    ➢ SM9: identity-based cryptography.
    Why develop national standards? Reduce dependence on foreign algorithms;
    support domestic certification and regulation; protect national information
    infrastructure; strengthen industrial and technological sovereignty; avoid blind
    trust in foreign standardization ecosystems. China does not live outside global
    cryptography. It uses, adapts and participates in international standards, but also
    promotes domestic algorithms for regulated and sovereignty-sensitive contexts.

  Final lesson: cryptography is a national-
  defence layer
    Cryptographic sovereignty means controlling what can be
    trusted. States shape cryptography through:
    •    export controls;
    •    product capabilities;
    •    exceptional-access proposals;
    •    hardware and APIs;
    •    certificate authorities;
    •    key recovery systems;
    •    standards and certification.
    Cryptographic sovereignty means the ability to choose, verify,
    implement, certify and replace standards and products that protect
    national infrastructure.
